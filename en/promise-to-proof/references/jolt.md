# The no-decision deal (JOLT)

Load when a deal is stalled, the buyer says "let me think about it", or there is no scheduled next step. Source framework: Dixon & McKenna, *The JOLT Effect*, built on analysis of about 2.5 million recorded sales conversations; they report that 40–60% of qualified opportunities end in no decision rather than a competitor win. Treat the percentage as their finding, not a universal law; measure your own no-decision rate (`references/metrics.md`).

## Why indecision is different

Two different fears drive a buyer:
- **Fear of missing out** (status quo is bad) — classic urgency and value arguments work.
- **Fear of messing up** (choosing wrong, overpaying, looking bad) — more urgency makes it *worse*. The buyer freezes.

Signals of fear of messing up: endless questions about edge cases, requests for more references after good ones, "we need to look at a few more options", meetings that end warmly without commitment, the champion going quiet after the proposal.

## The four moves

### J — Judge the level of indecision
Early, estimate how decisive this buyer is and adjust effort. Questions: "When you've bought something like this before, how long did it take from first look to signature?" "What usually slows decisions down here?" A buyer who took 14 months last time will not take 6 weeks now because you're persuasive.

### O — Offer a recommendation
Indecisive buyers want to be told, with confidence, by someone who has done this before. Narrow to 2–3 options maximum and recommend one: "Given what you told me about X and Y, I'd go with the standard plan and add the pilot clause. Here's why." Presenting six options to an anxious buyer is abandonment, not flexibility.

### L — Limit the exploration
Set boundaries on research. "What would you need to see to be comfortable deciding? Let's list it and I'll get you exactly that." Then hold the line: "You mentioned three things; we've covered all three. Is there anything new, or are we ready?" Also limit *your* scope creep: adding features to reassure an anxious buyer adds more things to worry about.

### T — Take risk off the table
Make the downside of a wrong choice small and visible: pilot with a defined exit, phased commitment, a guarantee that covers what you control (`references/guarantees-scarcity.md`), a reference from the same segment, your personal involvement in the first 30 days, clear "what happens if it doesn't work" language in the proposal.

## Scripts

- Opening the topic: "Can I be direct? I've seen this stage go two ways: teams move with a clear plan, or they keep evaluating and the problem costs them another quarter. What's making this hard to call?"
- When "we need more references": "Happy to. What would the next reference need to tell you that the last two didn't?"
- When silence after proposal: "I'm going to assume priorities shifted, which is normal. Is the problem still worth solving this year, or should I check back in Q2?" (a no-oriented question that gets a real answer)
- When the champion stalls: "What would your boss need to see in one page to say yes? Let's write that together." → `templates/business-case-onepager.md`

## What not to do

- Add urgency (deadlines, bonuses) to a buyer afraid of messing up.
- Expand the offer to "cover everything".
- Keep sending material nobody asked for.
- Let the deal sit in "proposal sent" for 30 days with no next step. Mark it at risk at 14 (`checklists/deal-review.md`).

## Measure it

Track no-decision as its own close reason. If it exceeds competitive losses, the fix is in this file, not in the pitch.
