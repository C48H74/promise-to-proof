# Guarantees, risk reversal, scarcity and urgency

Load when designing a guarantee, a refund policy, a pilot, or any deadline or capacity message.

## Guarantees: choose, don't stack by default

| Type | What it says | Exposure | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unconditional** | Refund within X days, no questions | Refund rate × price × volume | Low-touch products, trust-building for unknown brands |
| **Conditional** | Refund or continued work if the customer did their part and result Y didn't happen | Lower refund rate, higher rework cost | Programs where customer effort drives the result |
| **Process guarantee** | We deliver milestones A, B, C by dates; if not, Z | Rework cost | Services where outcome depends on the client |
| **Performance / pay-on-results** | Price tied to measured result | Revenue risk; needs an agreed baseline | Measurable, controllable outcomes |
| **Pilot with exit** | Paid, scoped trial with written success criteria and a decision date | Scoped cost | Enterprise, anxious buyers (`references/jolt.md`) |
| **None** | "All sales final" | Lower conversion | Custom work, consumables; say why |

**Exposure math before launch:** expected cost = probability of claim × cost per claim × volume. Add rework hours for conditional and process guarantees. If the expected cost exceeds the conversion gain, redesign.

Rules:
- **Guarantee only what you control**: your preparation, delivery, milestones, response times, and what you do if your part fails. Never guarantee a result that depends on the client's execution or their market.
- Stacking (short unconditional + long conditional) is an option for high-volume programs with known refund data; for a new offer, pick one and measure.
- **Name it** so it's remembered, and write it in plain terms. Vague "satisfaction guaranteed" is forgotten and litigated.
- Put the guarantee next to the price, not in the footer.

## Urgency: four legitimate reasons

1. **Cohorts and start dates** you actually run ("next group starts the 3rd").
2. **Seasonal windows** tied to the buyer's calendar (budget year-end, regulatory deadlines, peak season).
3. **Priced periods** (an entry condition valid until a date, rotated honestly; don't rename the same promotion every month and call it urgency).
4. **A closing window in the market** (a regulation, a tech shift, a competitor move) that is real and cited.

Urgency for a buyer afraid of choosing wrong backfires (`references/jolt.md`). Diagnose which fear you're dealing with first.

## Scarcity: real limits only

- **Capacity:** state what you can deliver ("8 clients per quarter; 2 slots left") only if true, and keep it true.
- **Limited editions and bonuses:** fixed quantities, honestly counted.
- **Per-period and per-cohort caps** in services double as retention tools (nobody wants to lose their slot).

When demand consistently exceeds capacity, raise price or add capacity; don't manufacture a waiting list.

## Trust check

Would you be comfortable if the buyer later saw your capacity sheet or your promotion calendar? If not, it isn't scarcity, it's a trick, and `references/psychology.md` applies.

## Anti-patterns

- Countdown timers on evergreen offers.
- Guarantees that promise revenue growth.
- "Only 3 spots" every week of the year.
- Stacked guarantees without refund data.

## Guarantee with exposure mathematics

Before promising any guarantee:

```
Exposure = (probability of being triggered) × (cost of honouring it)
```

- If `Exposure > tier margin` → **do not offer that guarantee**, or restrict the trigger.
- Defensible guarantees: redo the work at no cost **within the written scope** · return the difference against an agreed, auditable metric · exit period with notice · professional services included up to the value.
- **Never promise a result that depends on the client** (e.g., "you will make X in revenue").

Frame the guarantee as a **trust deposit** (see Trust check below), not as a disguised discount.
