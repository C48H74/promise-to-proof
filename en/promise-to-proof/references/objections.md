# Objections: diagnose before answering

Load for any objection. Scripts for the 15 most common: `objections/library.md`. Indecision has its own playbook: `references/jolt.md`. Price negotiation: `references/negotiation.md`.

## The method

An objection is a symptom. Answering the symptom (e.g., defending the price) usually makes it worse. Sequence:

1. **Slow down.** Silence for two seconds. Don't fill it.
2. **Label** what you heard without agreeing or arguing: "It sounds like the cost feels high relative to what you expect to get back."
3. **Diagnose** with one calibrated question: "What would need to be true for this to feel like a clear yes?" / "Compared to what?"
4. **Confirm** the real cause: "So the concern is less the number and more whether it will land with your CFO. Is that right?"
5. **Respond to the cause**, not the symptom, with evidence, a trade, or a changed scope.
6. **Check:** "Does that address it, or is there something else?"

If step 3 reveals a cause you can't fix (no budget authority anywhere, wrong fit), move to disqualification (`references/qualification.md`).

## Tools from tactical empathy (Voss)

- **Labeling:** "It seems like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…" — name the emotion or position, then stop talking.
- **Mirroring:** repeat the last 1–3 words as a question. "…too expensive?" The buyer elaborates.
- **Accusation audit:** say the negative before they do. "You might be thinking this is a lot for a company our size to ask for upfront."
- **Calibrated questions:** open questions starting with "how" or "what" that make the buyer solve the problem with you. "How would this get approved on your side?" "What about this doesn't work for you?"
- **No-oriented questions:** "Would it be a bad idea to involve finance now?" People say "no" more freely than "yes", and a "no" feels safe.

## Common objections and their usual real cause

| They say | Usual cause | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|
| "It's too expensive" | Value not quantified; comparing to the wrong alternative; budget cycle | "Expensive compared to what?" / "What were you expecting, and what was that based on?" |
| "I need to think about it" | Indecision: fear of choosing wrong, not of you | "What specifically would you be thinking about?" → `references/jolt.md` |
| "We already have a solution" | Status quo bias; switching cost | "What would it take for the current one to be good enough that we wouldn't be talking?" |
| "Send me some material" | Brush-off; no pain surfaced | "Happy to. So I send the right thing, what would you want it to answer?" |
| "Not a priority right now" | Implication never built | "Understood. What is the priority, and does this problem touch it?" |
| "I have to ask my boss / the committee" | You're single-threaded | "Makes sense. What will they ask you? Would it help if I joined?" → `references/multithreading.md` |
| "Can you do it cheaper?" | Testing; or a real constraint | "What would we need to take out to get there?" → `references/negotiation.md` |

## When to accept the no

Not every objection should be overcome. Accept and exit cleanly when:
- The root cause is a fit problem.
- The buyer has decided and is being polite.
- Overcoming it would require a promise you can't keep.

Exit script: "That makes sense. I'd rather you make the right call than close a deal that doesn't fit. If [trigger] changes, here's how to reach me." Leaving well is a referral strategy.

## Anti-patterns

- "I understand, but…" (the "but" erases the empathy).
- Answering an objection the buyer didn't raise.
- Discounting in response to "expensive" without a trade.
- Pressure ("the offer ends Friday") on a buyer who is afraid, not slow.

## Anatomy of an objection response

Every objection gets the same structure. Do not respond off the cuff.

```
OBJECTION:    [in the client's words]
WHAT IT IS:   [price | risk | timing | authority | inertia | indecision]
DIAGNOSIS:    [what is really behind it]
RESPONSE:     [acknowledge → return with a question → evidence → next step]
EVIDENCE:     [name, number or document — without this, do not use it]
TRAP:         [what NOT to say]
```

**Canonical order of the response:** acknowledge (without agreeing) → return with a question → only then evidence → propose the next step. **Never** start with the evidence: it sounds like a script.
