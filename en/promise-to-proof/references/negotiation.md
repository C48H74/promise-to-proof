# Negotiation and closing

Load for discount requests, procurement, contract terms, and any conversation where price is on the table. Closes with `templates/business-case-onepager.md` and `checklists/deal-review.md`.

## Prepare before you quote

- **Your walk-away:** the price or terms below which the deal destroys value for you (floor in `references/offer-pricing.md`).
- **Your alternatives:** other deals in pipeline. Nothing improves negotiation like a full pipeline.
- **Their alternatives:** status quo cost, competitors, in-house. Quantified.
- **Trade list:** things cheap for you and valuable to them (payment terms, start date, case study, referral, longer commitment, scope reductions) and the reverse.
- **Who decides on their side** and what their internal constraints are (budget cycle, approval thresholds).

## Anchor at the full price, then be quiet

State the price with the outcome attached, once, clearly: "The program is 18,000 for the six weeks, and it gets you [outcome]." Then stop. The first person to speak usually concedes. Don't justify, don't pre-discount, don't add "but we're flexible".

## Concessions are trades

Default rule: **never give without getting.** Formula: "I can do X if you can do Y."

| They ask | You trade for |
|---|---|
| 10% off | Annual prepayment, or a signed case study, or two referrals, or a start this month |
| Smaller scope at same price | No; smaller scope at a smaller price with the removed items named |
| Longer payment terms | Shorter contract exit for you, or a price step-up at renewal |
| Free pilot | Paid pilot credited against the contract, with written success criteria and a decision date |

**Never split the difference by default.** Meeting in the middle teaches the buyer that your first number was padding. If you move, move for a reason you can name.

## Counter-anchoring (adapted from Ackerman)

The Ackerman system was designed for buyers (open low, move 65% → 85% → 95% → 100% of target in shrinking steps, end on an odd number). Sellers face it in reverse. Defense:
- Expect their first number to be an anchor, not a limit. Don't react to it; label it: "That's a lot lower than the value we scoped. Help me understand what's driving that number."
- If you move, move in **shrinking steps** with a reason each time, and land on a precise figure (17,400, not 17,500) that signals calculation, not rounding.
- Your last step should include a non-price item so the final concession costs you little.

## Business case: the CFO is in the room

For any purchase that needs finance approval, write the one-pager *with* the champion: problem in numbers, cost of inaction, options considered, recommended option, investment, payback, risks and mitigations, decision needed by date. The champion sends it; you prepared it. Template: `templates/business-case-onepager.md`.

## Procurement and paper process

- Ask early: "What does procurement need from us, and how long does it usually take?" Security questionnaires, legal review and vendor onboarding are weeks, not days.
- Procurement's job is to reduce price after the business decision. Hold value with the business sponsor before procurement engages; agree with the champion what is non-negotiable.
- Standard procurement asks (payment terms, liability caps, exit clauses) are usually tradeable; price is not the only lever.

## Closing without manipulation

- Summarize what they said they wanted and how the proposal delivers it.
- Confirm the decision process is complete: "Is there anyone else who needs to see this before we sign?"
- Ask directly: "Shall we move forward?" Then silence.
- If yes: next steps with dates in the same conversation.
- If no: diagnose (`references/objections.md`), or exit cleanly.

## Red flags before you concede anything

- No scheduled next step.
- Single-threaded.
- No validated champion.
- "Verbal yes" with no mapped paper process.
- A discount request before the value conversation happened.

Any of these means the problem isn't price. Fix the flag; the price discussion gets easier.

## Anti-patterns

- Emailing a discount because someone asked by email.
- "What budget do you have?" as the first question.
- Conceding to procurement what the sponsor already accepted.
- Rounding down "to be nice".
